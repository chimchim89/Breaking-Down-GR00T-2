Breaking Down GR00T 2: NVIDIA's New Robot Foundation Model

November 10, 2026 · 6 min read

1. Introduction

On November 9, NVIDIA announced GR00T 2 at the Conference on Robot Learning in Austin.1 It's their newest robot foundation model, and it works with parallel grippers, dexterous robot hands and humanoids.2

Most of GR00T 2's training data isn't from robots. It's video of people doing their jobs, and in NVIDIA's tests that video made the robot better.2 I found that really interesting.

I went through NVIDIA's results, and five things stood out to me.

Before we start: I'm not a robotics person. I work in marketing, and I'm still learning this stuff. This is me trying to explain GR00T 2 in simple words, using what NVIDIA has shared. All the numbers come from NVIDIA (links are at the end). I haven't tested any of it myself. If I got something wrong or made it too simple, please tell me. I'd really like to fix it.

2. It Imagines Before It Acts

NVIDIA's previous model, GR00T 1.7, is a vision-language-action model, or VLA.4 It looks at the scene, reads the instruction, and works out a movement.

Before GR00T 2 moves, it first generates a short video of what should happen next. It plans its movements alongside that video, carries them out, looks at the scene again, and repeats.2

NVIDIA calls this a world action model, or WAM. The idea was introduced in DreamZero, a research paper NVIDIA published in February.3,5

According to NVIDIA, predicting how a scene should change is what lets GR00T 2 learn physics from human video.2 A person's video doesn't come with robot instructions. But it does show how things move, and GR00T 2 is trained to predict that.

3. Most of Its Training Data Is Human Video

GR00T 2 was pretrained on a mix of three kinds of data:2

  • 402,730 hours of video of people at work, filmed from their own point of view
  • 10,184 hours of demonstrations with a handheld gripper, across 2,897 tasks
  • 23,500 hours of robot teleoperation, where a person controls the robot to record examples

So human video makes up the large majority of what GR00T 2 learned from.

NVIDIA also checked whether more human video makes the robot better. They trained four versions of the model using none, 1%, 10% and 100% of the human video, then tested each one on 72 tasks on a real robot.2

NVIDIA has also made 100,000 hours of this human video openly available on Hugging Face, as a dataset called Human-1M-100K.2 That's the public part of it, not the full 402,730 hours.

4. It Can Do Tasks It Never Practiced

NVIDIA had people wearing gloves and head and wrist cameras demonstrate a few chemistry-lab tasks. Nobody ever demonstrated these tasks on the robot itself. The robot did get some general "play" data, which taught it how its own body looks and moves.2

On one task, lining a funnel with filter paper, GR00T 2 succeeded in 65% of trials. GR00T 1.7 succeeded in 0%.2

NVIDIA calls this X-Adapt, which means learning a skill on one kind of body and doing it on another. It showed this three ways:2

  • From people to robot hands: the filter paper task, at 65%.
  • From a handheld gripper to a robot arm: a YAM arm built a toy dump truck from start to finish in 60% of trials. The task takes up to 4 minutes, and the arm had no demonstrations of it.
  • From a wearable hand to robot hands: a two-handed robot prepared banana yogurt, scooped granola, and struck a match to light a lamp.

5. It Picks Up New Tasks Really Fast

NVIDIA tested GR00T 2 on 50 tasks the robot had never seen in its own data. They only showed up in human video or in other robots' data.2

Even with no examples, it succeeded at least once on 40 of the 50 tasks. With five examples, 37 of the 50 tasks succeeded in every single trial.

Those five examples add up to under 3 minutes of robot data. The retraining takes 30 minutes on a single RTX 5090.2

NVIDIA also looked at why the robot failed when it had no examples. Most of the time, it wasn't confused about what to do. It did the right steps in the right order, and one step just wasn't precise enough.2 NVIDIA says a little robot data closes that gap.

6. An AI Agent Can Be Its Manager

NVIDIA paired GR00T 2 with a reasoning AI model and split the job between them.2

The agent decides what to do. It breaks a long task into steps, checks progress, and writes code.

GR00T 2 decides how to do it. It handles the hands-on work that's hard to write code for, like gripping, fitting and inserting parts.

The agent keeps an eye on the robot. When a part drops or progress stalls, it fixes the scene and hands control back. It talks to GR00T 2 through what NVIDIA calls the WAM API.2

NVIDIA tested this on assembling toys. Every trial started by dumping all the parts onto the table, so the robot always began from a messy layout.2

Toy Fine-tuned Fine-tuned + DAgger With the agent
Toy plane 69 / 100 84 / 100 98 / 100
Toy tractor 51 / 100 71 / 100 97 / 100
Hot Wheels car 23 / 100 78 / 100 97 / 100

That's 292 out of 300 successful assemblies across all three toys.

There's also a smaller version called GR00T 2 Turbo. It uses a 2-billion-parameter model and is light enough to run on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot itself. Each prediction takes 124 milliseconds on the robot's onboard Jetson Thor.2 In NVIDIA's tests, the humanoid scooped and served popcorn in all 48 trials, and cleared a table of mixed objects in 93% of trials.

7. What's Not There Yet

There are some limits to keep in mind.

You can't use it yet. NVIDIA calls GR00T 2 open, but its research page says the code is coming in December.2 The announcement doesn't mention a license or a date for releasing the model itself.1

All the results come from NVIDIA. Every number in this piece is from NVIDIA's own tests. We don't know yet how it does when other teams try it on their own robots.

NVIDIA also lists some limits of its own.2

  • NVIDIA tried "visual steering", where the AI marks on the camera view where to grab an object and where to put it down. On the robot with two dexterous hands, this helped it place more items while sorting trash, but it rarely finished the whole task.
  • Those marks can't show how an object should end up facing, or motions like pouring and wiping. That still has to come from language instructions.
  • Fiddly insertion jobs, like plugging in a charger, started at 40 to 76% success. They only reached 88 to 96% after reinforcement learning on the real robot.

8. The Big Picture

Most of GR00T 2's training data is human video, and in NVIDIA's tests, adding more of it made the robot better every time.

The code is due in December. After that, people outside NVIDIA can try it on their own robots, and we'll learn more.
